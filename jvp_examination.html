📋 OSCE Station

Your next patient is a 42-year-old male presenting with chronic cough — follow-up visit. You are requested to assess Jugular Venous Pressure (JVP).

✅ Measure the JVP ✅ Explain steps loudly to assessor ❌ No history required
EXAMINATION STEPS
1
Preparation
Wash hands, introduce yourself, gain consent
▼
🗣 SAY TO ASSESSOR
"I will wash my hands, introduce myself to the patient, and confirm their name and date of birth.

I will explain that I need to examine the jugular venous pressure in the neck and obtain verbal consent."
✋ DO
  • Wash hands / use gel
  • Introduce yourself by name and role
  • Confirm patient identity
  • Explain the procedure briefly and gain consent
  • Expose the patient's neck and upper chest
  • Ensure adequate lighting
⭐ Exam Tip
In the OSCE, talk through every step out loud — the examiner marks you on communication, not just technical accuracy. Say "I am washing my hands" as you do it.
2
Positioning
Position the patient correctly at 45°
▼
🗣 SAY TO ASSESSOR
"I will position the patient at 45 degrees — this is the optimal angle for visualising the JVP.

I will ask the patient to turn their head 10–20 degrees to the left to relax the sternocleidomastoid muscle and improve visualisation of the internal jugular vein."
✋ DO
  • Sit patient at exactly 45° (use backrest or pillows)
  • Ask patient to turn head gently to the left (10–20°)
  • Remove pillow from under head — neck must be flat and relaxed
  • Stand on the patient's right side
⚠️ Why 45°?
  • At 0° (flat): JVP may not be visible if normal — vein collapses
  • At 90° (upright): JVP may be below clavicle — not visible
  • 45° gives the best window to see the meniscus of pulsation
⭐ Exam Tip
If the JVP is very elevated, you may need to sit the patient more upright (60–90°) to see the top of the column. Always state: "I am adjusting the angle to visualise the JVP better."
3
Identification
Locate the internal jugular vein pulsation
▼
🗣 SAY TO ASSESSOR
"I am looking for the internal jugular vein pulsation, which lies deep to the sternocleidomastoid muscle, between its two heads.

If I cannot identify the internal jugular pulsation, I will use the external jugular vein as an alternative — it is visible on the surface of the skin on the lateral neck."
✋ DO
  • Look at the right side of the neck (right IJV is preferred — more direct route to RA)
  • Identify the area between the two heads of the SCM muscle
  • Look for a double pulsation — JVP has a biphasic waveform (unlike the carotid which has a single beat)
  • JVP is non-palpable; carotid is palpable — this helps distinguish them
  • JVP is obliterated by gentle pressure above the clavicle; carotid is not
✅ JVP characteristics
  • Not palpable
  • Biphasic waveform (a and v waves)
  • Fills from above, empties downward
  • Decreases with inspiration (normally)
  • Obliterated by pressure above clavicle
❌ Carotid artery — NOT JVP
  • Palpable
  • Monophasic (single pulse)
  • Not obliterated by pressure
  • Not affected by respiration
  • Not affected by position change
⭐ Exam Tip — High Yield
The 3 key differences from carotid — memorise these: (1) JVP is non-palpable, (2) JVP is obliterated by pressure above clavicle, (3) JVP decreases with inspiration. Examiners love asking this.
4
Measurement
Measure the vertical height of JVP
▼
🗣 SAY TO ASSESSOR
"I will measure the JVP using the sternal angle (angle of Louis) as the fixed reference point — this is the bony ridge at the junction of the manubrium and the body of the sternum.

I place a horizontal ruler at the top of the visible JVP pulsation, and a vertical ruler down to the sternal angle. I measure the vertical distance in centimetres.

The sternal angle is approximately 5 cm above the right atrium, so I add 5 cm to this measurement to get the true CVP.

Normal JVP is 6–8 cm of water (i.e., 1–3 cm above the sternal angle at 45°)."
✋ DO
  • Identify the sternal angle (feel for the transverse ridge on the sternum)
  • Hold a horizontal ruler at the top of JVP pulsation
  • Hold a vertical ruler from the sternal angle upward to meet the horizontal ruler
  • Read the vertical distance in cm
  • Add 5 cm → this gives the CVP (central venous pressure)
📐 Normal Values
  • Normal JVP: 6–8 cm H₂O (1–3 cm above sternal angle)
  • <5 cm: Hypovolaemia — low circulating volume
  • >9 cm: Elevated — impaired cardiac filling, fluid overload
⭐ Exam Tip — High Yield
Common mistake: forgetting to add 5 cm. The sternal angle is 5 cm above the RA, so: CVP = measured height + 5 cm. If you see 3 cm above sternal angle → CVP = 8 cm (upper limit of normal).
5
Hepatojugular Reflux
Assess hepatojugular reflux (HJR)
▼
🗣 SAY TO ASSESSOR
"I will now perform the hepatojugular reflux test.

I will apply firm, sustained pressure over the right upper quadrant (liver) for 10–15 seconds while watching the JVP.

Normally, the JVP may rise transiently then return to baseline — this is a negative test.

If the JVP remains elevated throughout compression — more than 3 cm above baseline — this is a positive hepatojugular reflux, indicating right heart failure or fluid overload."
✅ Negative HJR (Normal)
  • Transient rise then returns to baseline
  • Right heart can accommodate increased venous return
🔴 Positive HJR (Abnormal)
  • JVP stays elevated >3 cm throughout
  • Suggests right heart failure, biventricular failure, or fluid overload
  • Also negative in SVC obstruction (hepatojugular reflex negative)
⭐ Exam Tip
HJR is negative in SVC obstruction — the vein is already fixed and distended with no pulsation, so applying pressure won't change anything. This is a key distinguishing feature.
6
Kussmaul's Sign
Check for Kussmaul's sign
▼
🗣 SAY TO ASSESSOR
"I will now check for Kussmaul's sign — I will ask the patient to take a slow deep breath in and observe the JVP.

Normally, the JVP decreases with inspiration — because negative intrathoracic pressure increases venous return and the right heart accommodates it.

Kussmaul's sign is positive if the JVP paradoxically rises with inspiration — this indicates impaired filling of the right ventricle."
💜 Kussmaul's Sign — Causes (Mnemonic: CROP)
  • Constrictive pericarditis
  • Restrictive cardiomyopathy
  • Obstruction — right heart failure (severe)
  • Pericardial effusion / tamponade
⭐ Exam Tip — High Yield
Kussmaul's vs. Pulsus Paradoxus: Both happen with inspiration and tamponade. Kussmaul's = JVP rises with inspiration. Pulsus paradoxus = BP drops >10 mmHg with inspiration. Tamponade can have both.
7
Conclusion
Report your findings to the assessor
▼
🗣 SAY TO ASSESSOR — Normal finding example
"On examination, the JVP is visible at [X] cm above the sternal angle with the patient at 45 degrees.

The waveform appears normal / biphasic with no cannon a waves, no prominent v waves, and no absent a waves observed.

The hepatojugular reflux is negative.

Kussmaul's sign is absent.

Overall, the JVP is within normal limits — approximately [total] cm of water, and I do not identify any features of right heart failure or SVC obstruction."
🗣 SAY TO ASSESSOR — Elevated JVP example
"On examination, the JVP is elevated at [X] cm above the sternal angle, giving a total CVP of approximately [X+5] cm H₂O.

[Describe any waveform abnormalities seen — cannon waves / absent a wave / prominent v wave]

The hepatojugular reflux is positive.

These findings are consistent with right heart failure / fluid overload and I would proceed to examine for other signs of cardiac failure."
JVP WAVEFORM
Wave What it represents Memory tip
a Right Atrial contraction — ends synchronously with carotid pulse Atrial contraction
c Right ventricular Contraction — triCuspid valve bulges toward right atrium triCuspid Closes
x Atrial relaXation + descent of tricuspid toward ventricle during systole (x' = measure of RV contractility) relaXation
v Venous filling — tricuspid valve is closed, venous return builds up pressure Venous filling
y Rapid emptYing of atrium when tricuspid valve opens emptYing
WAVEFORM ABNORMALITIES
🔴 Absent 'a' wave
  • Atrial fibrillation (no organised atrial systole)
🟡 Large 'a' wave
  • Tricuspid stenosis
  • Right heart failure
  • Pulmonary hypertension
🔴 Cannon 'a' wave
  • Atrium contracting against closed tricuspid valve
  • 3rd degree (complete) heart block
  • Ventricular tachycardia / ectopics
  • Atrial flutter
🟡 Large 'v' wave (c-v wave)
  • Tricuspid regurgitation — blood regurgitates back into atrium during systole
🟢 Absent 'x' descent
  • Tricuspid regurgitation (x wave replaced by positive wave)
🔵 Prominent 'x' descent (Friedreich's sign)
  • Constrictive pericarditis
💜 Slow 'y' descent
  • Tricuspid stenosis
  • Cardiac tamponade
🔵 Raised JVP, no pulsation
  • Superior vena cava (SVC) obstruction
  • HJR will be negative
6 MAIN CAUSES OF ELEVATED JVP
🧠 Mnemonic — "RF TC SA"
R F T C S A
RRight heart failure — most common (often 2° to LHF)
FFluid overload — excess intake or renal failure
TTricuspid regurgitation — massive v wave; JVP reflects RV pressure
CComplete heart block — AV dissociation → cannon a waves
SSVC obstruction — elevated JVP without pulsation; HJR negative
AAtrial fibrillation — no atrial systole → absent a wave
1. Right Heart Failure (most common)
  • Most common cause — often secondary to left heart failure
  • Secondary to ischaemic disease or mitral valve defects
2. Fluid Overload
  • Excess fluid intake or kidney failure (reduced excretion)
3. Tricuspid Regurgitation
  • Tricuspid valve does not close properly
  • JVP reflects right ventricular pressure — massive v wave on waveform
4. Complete Heart Block (3rd degree)
  • AV dissociation — atrial and ventricular contractions uncoupled
  • Atrium may contract against closed tricuspid → cannon a waves (irregular)
5. Superior Vena Cava (SVC) Obstruction
  • JVP elevated without pulsation
  • Hepatojugular reflex is negative
  • Usually caused by mediastinal lymphadenopathy / lung cancer
6. Atrial Fibrillation
  • No atrial systole → no 'a' wave
  • Irregular ventricular response
